Project 3

Where does our emissions choice matter most?

How much warmer each place gets than it was in 1995–2014, under a low- and a high-emissions future. climate models, 20-year averages. How we made this

The vertical axis stays fixed, so places can be compared. Click the chart to change the period.

About this graphic

What we found

  • The two futures look almost the same at first. Averaged over the globe, they are only 0.09°C apart in 2021–2040. That grows to 0.64°C in 2041–2060 and 2.83°C in 2081–2100 (+1.2°C on the low path versus +4.1°C on the high one). The emissions paths split in 2015, so most of the late-century difference comes from choices made decades before it shows up.
  • The Arctic has the biggest gap. North of the Arctic Circle, the 2081–2100 gap is 2.2 times the global one. The gap over land is also 1.6 times the gap over the ocean.
  • Land shows the difference before the ocean does. By 2041–2060 the gap is clear over 48% of the land but only 3% of the ocean. For the global average it becomes clear in 2046–2065.
  • A few places never get a clear answer. South of Greenland, 3 of the 18 models actually warm less on the high path, and the gap there does not become clear by 2100. About 1% of the globe is like this.
  • San Diego's gap (+2.9°C) turned out to be almost the same as the global average.

Design decisions

Visual encodings

  • Equal Earth projection. We wanted the amount of dark red on the map to match how much of the planet it covers, so we needed an equal-area projection. Mercator or a plain latitude–longitude grid would stretch the high latitudes, and that is where our biggest values are.
  • One colour scale for the three temperature views. Low, high and gap all use the same eight classes, so a colour means the same number of degrees in every view and you can compare them by switching back and forth. Anything within ±0.5°C is grey for “about no change”, and blue is only used for cooling. We used binned colours instead of a continuous ramp because they are easier to read off the legend, and because you can see the class boundaries move when the animation plays.
  • A different scale for “When it shows”. This view shows time, not temperature, so we gave it its own single-hue blue scale (darker means sooner). That way it does not get confused with degrees.
  • Smoothing. The data is on a 2.5° grid, which looks blocky, so we draw it pixel by pixel with bilinear interpolation. The tooltip uses the same interpolation, so the number under the cursor matches the colour you see.
  • The line chart. High emissions is always orange and low emissions is always blue. The shaded band around each line covers the middle 80% of models. We fixed the vertical axis so that a place with an 8°C gap looks bigger than one with 3°C. A vertical line marks the period when that place's gap becomes clear.

Interaction

  • Four views change what the map shows: each future on its own, the gap between them, and when the gap becomes clear.
  • The slider and Play button step through the 20-year periods. The animation is the best way to see the timing. The gap map stays mostly grey until mid-century and then fills in starting from the north.
  • Hover to get exact values and the model range. Click to pin a place and see its chart. The place buttons jump to a few locations we thought were interesting, including San Diego. Clicking on the chart also moves the map to that period, so the map and chart stay in sync.
  • The notes on the map are recalculated for every view and period. A note is hidden when what it says is no longer true for the current view, so they do not go out of date.

Alternatives we considered

  • Three maps side by side. At normal web widths each map would be too small to see the 2.5° detail, so we went with one big map and a toggle.
  • A blue–red scale centred on zero. Almost every value is warming, so half of the colours would have gone unused.
  • One frame per year. Every frame is already a 20-year average, so yearly frames would be five times the data and look basically the same.
  • A line for every model. We tried this, but eighteen overlapping lines per future were unreadable, so we kept the band.

Data and transformations

  1. Source. Monthly near-surface air temperature (tas) from the CMIP6 archive on Google Cloud. We used the historical runs plus the SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios.
  2. Models. We took one model per institution so that no modelling centre gets counted twice, and one run per model that is available in all three experiments. That left us with 18 models: .
  3. Annual means. We averaged the months into years, weighting each month by its number of days.
  4. A common grid. The models all use different grids, so we interpolated each one to 2.5° (144 by 72 cells). To check this, we compared each model's global average on the new grid with its original grid, and they agree to within 0.012°C.
  5. Warming. Each model is compared with its own 1995–2014 average. This takes out the differences in where each model's climate starts.
  6. 20-year averages, stepped every 5 years. The period labelled 2081–2100 is the average of exactly those years, so 2021–2040, 2041–2060 and 2081–2100 line up with the periods the IPCC uses.
  7. Ensemble. The map shows the simple average of the 18 models. The bands and tooltip ranges are the 10th to 90th percentile across models.
  8. The gap is high minus low. We calculate it inside each model first and then average, so the range shows how much the models disagree about the gap itself.
  9. “Clear” means the 10th percentile of the gap is above 0.5°C (so 9 out of 10 models agree it is at least that big) and it stays above that in every later period. We chose 0.5°C as a meaningful threshold for identifying when the difference between the two emissions scenarios becomes substantial and sustained. This is a visualization threshold, rather than a formal statistical significance test.

Caveats. These are raw model averages. Our set includes some high-sensitivity models, so it runs warmer than the IPCC's assessed ranges, which also use other lines of evidence. The bands show how spread out the models are and should not be read as probabilities. The code that prepares the data is in project3/pipeline.

Time: ~20 hours

What took the most time. Preparing the data took the longest. Downloading the 54 model runs was quick, but checking them was not. Three of the runs had their 20-year files stored out of time order, so we had to sort them. When we compared every model's global average before and after regridding, we also found that one model (FGOALS-g3) has unevenly spaced latitudes, which a simple area weighting gets wrong. After that, the map renderer took the most work. We had to draw the grid pixel by pixel through the projection, and we hit a bug where D3 wraps longitudes and paints colour outside the globe. Testing the finished page turned up some smaller problems too, like a legend that collapsed and a tooltip that showed old values while the animation was playing.

Tools and sources

All of the plotting is done with D3 v7.9.0. We only use topojson-client to decode the country outlines, which come from Natural Earth through world-atlas. The data was prepared in Python with xarray, zarr and gcsfs.
